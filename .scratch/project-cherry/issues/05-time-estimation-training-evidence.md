# Evidence on training time estimation in ADHD

Type: research
Status: resolved

## Question

What does the research literature say about time blindness / time perception deficits in ADHD and whether time estimation can be trained? Specifically: does prospective estimate + immediate feedback on actual duration improve calibration; is retrospective 'felt duration' vs clock time a useful signal; do coarse buckets vs precise numbers matter; what feedback framing avoids shame/avoidance; any evidence on reason attribution (why was I off). Prefer peer-reviewed sources and note evidence strength honestly.

## Answer

Full findings: [`research/time-estimation-training-evidence.md`](https://github.com/Jakeplusplus/project-cherry/blob/research/time-estimation-training-evidence/research/time-estimation-training-evidence.md) on branch `research/time-estimation-training-evidence`.

- **The deficit is real, but shown at lab scale.** Timing differences in ADHD are well replicated for milliseconds to about a minute (Marx et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2022). The pattern is "less accurate and more variable", not uniform underestimation. Adult evidence is mixed; real-task evidence at minute scale is one 40-person study (Prevatt et al. 2011).
- **No direct evidence that this app's loop trains time estimation in ADHD.** No controlled study of estimate-then-feedback training for task durations in ADHD was found. Closest: a 38-child RCT of a multi-part coached programme with small gains the children themselves did not report (Wennberg et al. 2018). A 22-adult study of 30 days of time-estimation practice found accuracy got worse (Fontes et al. 2020).
- **Feedback helps mainly when the real number is shown at the next estimate, for a similar task** (general population: Roy et al. 2008; Thomas & König 2018 failed to replicate feedback alone, only same-task experience helped). People misremember past durations as shorter and explain away misses.
- **Felt duration:** one supporting lab study as a learning aid (König et al. 2015); no ADHD evidence.
- **Buckets vs precise numbers:** untested for calibration learning; indirect evidence that coarser units push estimates upward (Jørgensen 2016).
- **Framing:** no time-specific ADHD evidence. Feedback aimed at the self backfires (Kluger & DeNisi 1996); adults with ADHD show low self-compassion and a history of criticism (Beaton et al. 2022).
- **Reason attribution:** discouraging — structured reflection did not improve estimates (Jørgensen & Gruschke 2009), and attribution is how people dismiss misses as one-offs.
- **Design implications (researcher's inference, not findings):** position the app as "see your real numbers" rather than "train your time sense"; the similar-task hint at estimate time is the best-supported piece and deserves more than "very thin"; keep the retro light; report bucket hit/miss rather than implying minute-level calibration; make "why was I off" optional tags or leave it out of v1.

**Biggest caveat:** nearly everything usable is extrapolated — from second-scale lab timing to hour-scale real tasks, from students and software engineers to adults with ADHD, from single sessions to months of use. Whether the loop trains anything is untested. Verification gaps (NICE NG87 page not loaded; two papers citation-confirmed only; one via automated summary) are listed in the file.
